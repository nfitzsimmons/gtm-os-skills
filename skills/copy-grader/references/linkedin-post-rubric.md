# LinkedIn thought-post rubric

> **Load when:** grading a LinkedIn (or LinkedIn-style) thought, educational, or point-of-view post in Mode A.
> **Do NOT load when:** grading a promotional post with a real offer and CTA, such as a product launch, a gated asset, or a comment-a-keyword lead magnet. Those have a hard offer and a next action, so `default-rubric.md` scores them correctly.

Eleven weighted principles for a thought-leadership post, retuned from `default-rubric.md`. The change that matters: a thought post has **no hard offer and no sales CTA**, so those two default principles (offer 9, CTA 6) are removed instead of scored at full weight and capped. A post that argues a point and ends on a question or a stake is doing its job, and penalizing it for lacking a "Get my report" button measures the wrong thing. The freed weight moves to the levers that carry a post: the hook, the single idea, the proof, and the felt stake. One low-weight principle (portable takeaway) keeps the post honest about leaving the reader something repeatable, without bringing the offer trap back.

Weights sum to 100. Score each 0 to 100 with quoted evidence. Weighted total = Σ(score × weight) / 100. The examples below illustrate the principle and are not real data.

| 1 | Hook earns the read | 18 |
| 2 | One clear idea | 12 |
| 3 | Specific over vague | 12 |
| 4 | Believable and proof-backed | 11 |
| 5 | Reader-relevant (their stake, not a monologue) | 9 |

| 8 | Clarity over cleverness | 7 |
| 9 | Portable takeaway (the saveable idea) | 6 |

## The principles

**1. Hook earns the read (18).** The first line makes scrolling past it cost something. LinkedIn shows roughly the first 210 characters before "see more," so the open carries even more weight than in long form. *Good:* "Our best-converting page last quarter was the one the design team never touched." *Bad:* "I've been thinking a lot about AI lately." Highest weight on the page, because a weak hook caps the whole post.

**2. One clear idea (12).** The post makes a single argument the reader can restate in a sentence. *Good:* "Pick the conversion event before you pick the keywords." *Bad:* a post that pitches a framework, a tool, and a hot take in one breath. A thought post that forks reads as two drafts wearing one coat.

**3. Specific over vague (12).** Named numbers, tools, moments, and scars beat adjectives, and they're what only this author could have written. *Good:* "I cut 14 of our 22 lifecycle emails and pipeline didn't move." *Bad:* "I made some changes to my process." Vagueness is how a competent post stays forgettable, and on LinkedIn it also reads as someone who didn't do the work.

**4. Believable and proof-backed (11).** The claim rests on something real: a mechanism, a named artifact, or a before and after the author lived. *Good:* a literal before and after of a rule the author changed. *Bad:* "This approach works wonders." An unbacked assertion scores low even when it's grammatically clean. Flag any statistic you can't trace to a source.

**5. Reader-relevant (9).** The post speaks to the reader's stake or field, not just the author narrating their week. First person is fine and expected, but the reader should see themselves in it. *Good:* a line that names the reader's situation in their own terms. *Bad:* a diary entry with no door for the reader to walk through.

**6. Emotional resonance (9).** The post touches the real feeling under the topic, such as the frustration of a tool that keeps lying to you, or the relief of a fix that finally holds. *Good:* a concrete scene the reader has lived. *Bad:* a neutral how-to with no stakes.

**7. Momentum (8).** Every line creates a reason to read the next (Sugarman's slippery slide). On a phone this is survival: a strong first paragraph, open loops, and no dead paragraph that becomes the last one read.

**8. Clarity over cleverness (7).** Understood on one read, with no decoding. *Good:* plain words doing precise work. *Bad:* a pun or a too-clever frame that costs comprehension.

**9. Portable takeaway (6).** The post leaves the reader one repeatable idea they can apply or quote: a test, a rule, or a reframe. This replaces the default rubric's offer principle at lower weight, because a thought post's offer is the insight, not a CTA. *Good:* "If a script can check it without judgment, it belongs in code, not in the prompt." *Bad:* a post that's interesting in the moment and leaves nothing to carry. Do **not** penalize a missing sales CTA here. Reward the presence of a usable idea.

**10. Voice fidelity (5).** It sounds like the author, not generic high-engagement LinkedIn copy. Weighted higher than in the default rubric because a personal post lives or dies on sounding like a person. A rewrite that flattens voice to chase the other principles has failed (see the hard rules in `SKILL.md`).

**11. Rhythm and readability (3).** Sentence length varies, it scans on a phone, and the whitespace breathes. Paragraphs of two to four sentences read as written by a person. A one-line paragraph works for the hook or the close. *Bad:* metronome pacing, a wall of text, or a stack of one-line beats.

## Notes

- There is deliberately **no CTA principle**. A thought post can end on a question, a stake, or a flat statement. Engagement bait ("Agree? Comment below") should *lose* points under voice fidelity, not earn them under a CTA line.
- For a promotional post with a genuine offer and next action, use `default-rubric.md`, which scores offer and CTA at full weight.
